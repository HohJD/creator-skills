# Editing a voiceover

Read the rough take as if the creator were beside you, showing you the screen. Notice their vocabulary and where they pause to let you look. An unusual phrase they chose can carry more of their voice than a page of tone settings.

## Two edits, two different jobs

These examples use invented footage notes and dialogue.

**A line that needs translating into speech.** The screen shows a second heading called “Email” halfway down a combined spreadsheet. The draft says, “Following the consolidation of our datasets, we can observe the presence of a redundant header.”

A recordable line is “See ‘Email’ halfway down? That's where the second sheet starts.” Let the cursor land on the cell as the narrator says “Email.” The word gives the listener somewhere to look. If the creator only showed the problem, end there. A line about fixing it needs a fix the viewer can see.

**A line that needs leaving alone.** The creator says, “Oh, there's Tuesday. I've been staring at Monday.” The footage shows them switching between those tabs. Keep the line. “I realised I had selected the incorrect tab” loses the discovery and takes longer to say. If the tabs aren't legible, fix the crop in the shot plan.

## The last pass before recording

- Listen for a sentence that asks the speaker to introduce a term, give an instruction and explain the result in one breath. Put the instruction on the action. Move the explanation to the moment the result appears.
- Keep the exact flag, command or button label in the shot notes where someone can copy it. In the voiceover, give the listener the words they'd actually say. Keep a label in the narration when it helps them find the right control.
- Give pauses a job. If the viewer needs to hear the original audio or read a changed value, leave space for it. A quiet ending can work.
- Match the creator's register. Keep their contractions, fragments, humour or regional phrasing where they make sense. Adding slang, filler words or a stock confession can make a perfectly good take sound like somebody else.
- For a duration target, rehearse at the creator's pace and include the screen actions. Label a duration as an estimate until someone's timed the take. For bilingual work, pace each language on its own.

For a personal voice profile, keep track of who wrote each sample. Approval alone tells you a line was acceptable. A creator changing “utilise” to “use” tells you something more specific about their preference.
