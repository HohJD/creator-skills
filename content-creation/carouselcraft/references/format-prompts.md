# Eight carousel formats with prompts

Pick the structure that fits what you actually have. Bracketed words are fields to replace. Each block works as a standalone request. Each asks for two distinct outputs: the words that appear on the card and the instructions for making the visual.

## 1. Tutorial

A reader wants to do one thing. Show the destination, the steps in order, then a result they can check.

> Make a [slide count]-card tutorial for [audience] on [task]. Use these tested steps and observations: [notes]. Open on the result. Put one action on each middle card and finish with how I can tell it worked. Give me exact card copy and a matching visual prompt per card. For an interface step, specify the crop I need from my real screenshot.

## 2. Checklist

A reader wants something they can keep open while they work. Use checks with clear pass conditions. End with a compact checklist worth saving.

> Turn [workflow or notes] into a [slide count]-card preflight checklist for [audience]. Group the checks by when I'd use them. Write each as something I can see, hear or confirm. End with a compact checklist I could screenshot. Supply the card copy and a layout prompt for each card. Make checked and unchecked states readable without relying on colour.

## 3. Side-by-side comparison

A reader is choosing between two options. Compare them against the same criteria and show when the choice changes.

> Help [audience] choose between [A] and [B] using these observations: [facts]. Make a comparison carousel. Put both options on each criteria card in the same order. Close with “choose A if…” and “choose B if…” grounded in those facts. Give exact card copy and a visual prompt per slide with matching scale and labels for both options. Leave unknowns explicit.

## 4. Before and after

A reader wants to understand one change. Use matched views, point to the edit and explain what it changed.

> Build a before-and-after carousel from [original], [revision] and [what changed]. Start by inviting the reader to spot the difference. Show the same detail at the same scale, reveal the edit, then give one principle they can reuse. Include exact slide copy and crop or layout prompts for each card. Use the supplied originals as evidence.

## 5. Mistake and fix

A reader recognises a symptom. Show how to distinguish a possible cause, make a correction and check it.

> Write a [slide count]-card troubleshooting carousel about [visible symptom] for [audience]. Use [known cause, evidence and tested fix]. Start with what the reader would notice. Show one diagnostic check, the correction, then a way to verify it. Include publishable copy and a visual prompt for each card. If the cause is uncertain, frame it as a test.

## 6. Photo story or case study

A reader follows a decision someone made. Build the sequence from actual moments and let the strongest image carry a short caption.

> Sequence [photos or field notes] into a story about [decision or turning point]. Open on one concrete moment, show what led to it, then show what happened afterwards. Use only details in my material. Give me exact captions and a photo selection, crop or illustration prompt for each card. Mark illustrations clearly when they stand in for a scene.

## 7. Annotated teardown

A reader wants to learn how an existing piece works. Show the whole object first, then point to particular details, then reassemble the lesson.

> Make a teardown of [supplied design, post or artifact] for [audience]. Show the whole piece, then zoom in on [details] one at a time. Pair each visible detail with a lesson someone could apply. Give me the exact slide copy and annotation prompts with crop, pointer placement and label position. Use the original artifact throughout.

## 8. Prompt pack

A reader wants reusable prompts for a particular job. Give them distinct tasks, fill-in fields and a way to judge the output.

> Build a prompt-pack carousel for [audience] doing [job]. Include [number] distinct prompts for [tasks]. Give each prompt its own card with a short purpose and the exact prompt readers can copy, including the inputs they should paste. Close with one way to check their result. Separately supply a layout prompt per card. Keep prompts as selectable or typeset text and include a plain-text copy if requested.

## Turning a visual direction into an image prompt

For illustration-led slides, a reusable starting point is:

> Make a [ratio] [medium] illustration of [specific subject and action]. Use [palette] and [lighting or mark-making]. Frame [detail] on the [side] and leave [area] empty for the headline. No lettering or logos.

For example, a checklist about packing pottery might use this on the padding card:

> Portrait 4:5 overhead ink illustration. A pair of hands slips folded kraft paper between two stacked ceramic bowls. Dark indigo linework, warm ivory ground, one rust accent. Place the bowls in the lower half and leave the upper third clear for editable copy. No lettering.

A comparison using real packing photos needs a different direction: “Crop both supplied photos to the same bowl rim. Put the unpadded stack on the left and the padded stack on the right. Place editable labels immediately below each photo.” This preserves the evidence behind the comparison.
